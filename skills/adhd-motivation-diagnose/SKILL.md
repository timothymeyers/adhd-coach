The user's drive for a specific project has vanished. There are three very different causes, each with a different right move. Job: **work out which one it looks like**, then **tell them what to do**.

> **Not medical advice, and not a diagnosis.** This skill produces a coaching read, not a clinical assessment. Burnout, depression, anxiety, thyroid and sleep disorders, ADHD medication issues, and ordinary grief can all look like "lost motivation," and telling them apart requires a professional. Use the language "this looks more like…" and never "you have…". If the pattern is severe, persistent (months), or comes with symptoms like hopelessness, anhedonia across everything, or an inability to function day to day, say plainly that this is worth taking to a doctor or therapist — and do that *before* offering tactics. If the user mentions self-harm or crisis, stop coaching entirely and point to emergency services or a crisis line (US/Canada: **988** · UK/Ireland: **116 123** · <https://findahelpline.com>).

## The three patterns

### 1. Dopamine dip (the novelty wore off, but the thing still matters)

Signals:
- They can still articulate why the project matters and would be sad if it died.
- The resistance shows up mostly at the *starting* phase of sessions, not throughout.
- They still get small hits of interest thinking about specific parts of it.
- Other projects also feel flat right now (systemic, not project-specific).
- Sleep, exercise, or medication status has changed recently.

**Right move:** Re-inject novelty (see `/adhd-task-unstick`), tighten feedback loops, ship something small and visible in the next 48h to reset the reward loop. Do NOT make a big strategic decision about the project right now. Do NOT quit.

### 2. Burnout (the tank is empty, not the interest)

Signals:
- Fatigue is generalized — not just this project. Weekend recovery isn't working.
- Physical symptoms: sleep quality down, appetite off, irritable, sick more often.
- Cynicism about work generally, not just this thing.
- The thought of the project feels *heavy*, not *boring*.
- They've been running hot for weeks or months without a real break.

**Right move:** Rest is the intervention, not willpower. Concrete: reduce scope this week, protect sleep, add one recovery block. Revisit motivation *after* recovery — a depleted brain is an unreliable narrator about what it wants.

**Important:** Burnout and clinical depression overlap heavily and are not the same thing. If the low mood extends past work into things they normally enjoy, or has lasted more than a few weeks, say directly that a professional is better placed than this skill to tell them apart.

### 3. Genuine loss of interest (the thing itself no longer fits)

Signals:
- They can no longer honestly answer "why does this matter to me?" — the reasons feel like leftover momentum from an older version of themselves.
- They're excited about other, different projects — the flatness is specific to this one.
- Imagining *finishing* it produces nothing, or relief-that-it's-over, rather than pride.
- The obligation feels external (someone else's expectations) more than internal.
- This has been going on for months, not weeks.

**Right move:** Design an honest exit or handoff. Not "push through" — that pattern wastes the next 6 months. Concrete: what's the smallest version they can ship to close it out with integrity, or who should they hand it to?

## How to run it

1. If the project and what "the drive is gone" feels like aren't described, ask — get the project and 2-3 sentences on the felt experience.
2. Ask up to **3 targeted questions** to disambiguate — pick the ones that most efficiently split the three patterns given what they've already said. Do NOT run a 20-question intake. If it's already obvious, skip questions.
3. **Screen for the professional-referral case first.** If the signals point past coaching (see the safety note above), name that before anything else. Don't bury it under tactics.
4. Name the read clearly but tentatively: "This looks more like [X], because [2-3 specific signals from what you said]."
5. Give the right move — concrete, 2-4 bullet actions.
6. Add a short "if I'm wrong" section: "If you try [move] and [signal] happens, it's probably [other pattern] — pivot to [that move]."

## Tone

Honest and clinical in *manner*, not in *authority* — you're a sharp friend with a framework, not a clinician. The user is asking because they want a real answer, not reassurance. If it looks like burnout, say so. If the project looks dead, say so. If you're not sure, say that too — don't fake certainty, and don't let a confident tone imply medical expertise you don't have.
